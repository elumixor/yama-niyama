Yama & Niyama

Yama · The First Observance · 01

अहिंसा

Ahiṃsá

Non-harming

Not inflicting pain by thought, word or action. Not the refusal to ever take life — the refusal of the intention to harm.

Scroll

The word

To cause pain to none — in thought, in word, in deed.

Manovákkáyaeh sarvabhútánámapiidánamahiṃsá.

हिंसा

Hiṃsá

Any thought or action carried out with the intention of causing harm to another. The mark is not the outcome — it is the will behind the act.

अहिंसा

Ahiṃsá

To guide your conduct so that your thought and action cause pain to nobody, and are unjust to none. The whole of the discipline turns on that one word: intention.

This word is wrongly interpreted by many. Read it strictly and you cannot live in a society, a forest, or a cave.

The impossible reading

If killing alone were the sin, no one could draw breath.

Some so-called learned people forbade the plough, since tilling crushes the creatures under the soil. They poured sugar into ant-hills while human beings went hungry. But respiration kills numberless microbes; medicine destroys the bacteria of disease; even filtering water means killing what lives in it. Followed to its end, this reading leaves nowhere to stand. It is no definition of ahiṃsá — it is against the very laws of existence.

The law of existence

Life lives on life.

Rice is got by killing the paddy — and paddy can sprout, paddy can reproduce; there is life in it too. To eat is to destroy lower forms, whether or not you mean any harm. Such acts are done to maintain life, not to inflict pain, and so they are not hiṃsá. Where there is no intention to harm, the question of hiṃsá does not arise at all.

The aggressor — átatáyii

Kśetradárápahárii ca shastradhárii dhanápaháh / Agnidagaradashcaeva śadete hyátatáyinah

  • iSeizes your land by force
  • iiAbducts your wife
  • iiiComes with a weapon to kill you
  • ivSnatches away your wealth
  • vSets fire to your house
  • viTakes life by administering poison

To use force against such a one is not against ahiṃsá. Hiṃsá and the use of force are not the same thing.

Valour

The use of force against an aggressor is valour; desisting from it is cowardice.

Heroism shows itself in the fight against aggressors. Ráma waged war on Rávaṇa — not to conquer Laṅká, but to recover what was stolen. Krṣṇa pressed the Pándavas to take up arms against usurpers. Maháprabhu, the very incarnation of mercy, pounced like a lion on the tyrant judge. To hide weakness behind the word ahiṃsá may serve a political end, but it does not protect the sanctity of righteousness.

Two tests before you act

Correction is not harm. But neither is courage the same as folly.

The intention

Correction is not hiṃsá

When a parent punishes a child, the aim is not to make it weep — it is to correct. Any act done in the true spirit of rectification, however harsh it seems, is not hiṃsá. To pardon an aggressor before reforming them only encourages injustice.

The strength

Measure your power first

The weak must assess their strength before fighting a powerful aggressor. History calls the opposite error “Rájput folly” — to charge valiantly, again and again, without ever counting the cost, and so to lose every battle and die a heroic death.

So gain the power to resist evil — then make proper use of it. Inability is no virtue.

Where it leads

Guard every life, including the one you must defend.

Ahiṃsá is the first of the five Yama — regulated conduct toward the world. It does not ask you to be harmless; it asks you to be just. Once the intention to harm is gone, what remains is Satya, the next observance: not bare fact, but truth bent toward the welfare of all.
