Yama & Niyama

Yama · The Second Principle · 02

सत्य

Satya

Benevolent truthfulness

Truth is not the bare fact. It is the word and the thought used for the welfare of all — and that has no English name.

Scroll

The word

Two words the English language keeps confusing.

ऋत

Rta

The bare fact. To report a thing exactly as it stands — accurate, literal, indifferent to what the report will do. This is what most people mean by “truth.” The sādhaka is not asked to follow it.

सत्य

Satya

The proper action of mind and the right use of words, with the spirit of welfare. Its practical side bends with circumstance; its finality rests in Parama Brahma. Fact serves the moment. Satya serves the good.

You can state every fact correctly and still betray the truth.

The definition

Parahitártham váunmanaso
yathárthatvaṃ satyam.

Parahitártham váunmanaso yathárthatvaṃ satyam.

“Satya is the correct use of mind and words, done for the benefit of others.”

The refugee

A terrified man hides in your house.

You may know for certain he is innocent. A ruffian follows, bent on torturing him, and comes to your door asking where he is. Tell the plain fact and the man is murdered — and you, through your “truth,” become indirectly guilty of the crime. Here, fact and welfare point in opposite directions.

The ruffian asks: where is he?

Two answers. Both leave your lips. Only one is Satya.

Rta · the fact “He is here, hiding inside.”
Satya · the welfare “No one came this way. Look elsewhere.”

To misguide the aggressor so the innocent may safely return home — that is the higher truth.

The letter

Your mother is eating when the news arrives.

The letter says her father has died. She asks what it contains. Blurt the fact and the shock takes her food, perhaps her strength. So you say all is well at home; you let her finish; later you mention his illness, and prepare the ground gently. Something other than fact was uttered — and the dignity of Satya was kept whole.

How it holds together

One foot in the moment, the other in the Absolute.

The practical side

Relativity

What welfare asks shifts with person, place and time. The sādhaka, dealing with the relativity of their surroundings, must judge each case by one measure: what is genuinely good for humanity. The words may change; the spirit does not.

The finality

Parama Brahma

Satyaṃ jiṋánam anantaṃ Brahma.

Truth, knowledge, infinity — that is Brahma. The finality of Satya does not rest in any fact at all, but in the Supreme Entity, so often called the very essence of Satya.

Satya is anchored not in what is said, but in what it is said for.

The spirit of welfare

Parahitártham — for the benefit of others.

This is the hinge of the whole principle. Humans are rational beings, able in varying degrees to do what is good for humanity. Thought, word and action turned toward that good — that, and not the literalness of the words, is what the discourse defines as Satya.

Where it leads

Every fact is relative. Truth is not.

Satya begins in the give-and-take of daily life, where the welfare of others guides each word — and it ends in Parama Brahma, the one entity that is itself the essence of truth. Where Ahiṃsá guards others from your harm, Satya turns your very speech into their good.
