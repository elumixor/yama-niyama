Yama & Niyama

Yama · The Third Principle · 03

अस्तेय

Asteya

Non-stealing

Not taking what belongs to another — not by force, not by plan, not by quietly withholding what is owed.

Scroll

The word

Steya is theft. Asteya is its undoing.

स्तेय

Steya

To take possession of what belongs to others — whether by an armed hand, by brute strength, or by a clever turn of intellect. The thief is not only the one who flees with the loot.

अस्तेय

Asteya

The settled refusal to deprive — in act and in intention. Honest exchange is never theft: to give money for crops or gold in the proper way takes nothing from anyone.

Paradravyápaharańa tyágo'steyam. To relinquish the taking of others' property — that is asteya.

The line is drawn not at the act, but at the intention to deprive.

The reckoning

Theft is fourfold.

01

Physical theft

Taking a material object — money or goods — by force of arms, of muscle, or of intellect. Behind the act lies the intention of taking another's property by deceit.

02

Psychic theft

You took nothing, but you planned it in the mind. Only the fear of the law, or of being criticised, held your hand. The theft was already committed inwardly.

03

Depriving others of their due

You possess nothing of theirs, yet you withhold what is rightfully owed. Their loss is on your account. That too is stealing.

04

Planning that deprivation

You did not actually withhold anyone's due — but you intended to. The plan alone is theft, because asteya breaks in the mind before the hand ever moves.

The barter you forgot

Riding without a ticket is theft.

You pocket no cash from the railway, but a ticket is your half of a bargain — you took the facility and never paid for it. Educated people do it; missionaries do it; leaders with noble purposes do it. They criticise corruption loudly, then ride for a few rupees less. It is not only theft. It is also pettiness.

The two faces

One shop, two grades of honesty.

To his friends

The genuine article

The shopkeeper sets aside the real, unadulterated goods. These he gives to his guests, his friends, the people he must look in the eye.

To his customers

The adulterated

To the stranger across the counter — and to the well-to-do, and to the government — he hands the diluted, the cut, the false. He has decided some people may be cheated.

Any deal carried out with this background is against asteya. There is no class of person it is fair to rob.

What asteya is not

It does not forbid honest trade.

Accepting money, crops, or gold in fair exchange takes nothing from anyone — the scale stays level. The railway, having charged the full fare, owes you no free service, and you owe it the fare. Asteya does not shrink the marketplace. It only insists that every weight on the scale be true.

The cure

Plant the code before temptation arrives.

The easiest way to practise asteya is auto-suggestion. If, from childhood, a person keeps reminding themselves what is correct, they do not go astray once grown — even in the midst of temptation. Character is the habit rehearsed long before it was tested.

Where it leads

Owe no one, and the mind is light to carry.

Asteya is the ledger kept honest down to the few rupees. Where Satya guards what you say and Ahiṃsá guards what you do, asteya guards what you quietly take. Settle every due, abandon even the plan to deprive, and the mind is freed of the small corruptions that crude it — clear now for Brahmacarya, the seeing of Brahma in every thing.
